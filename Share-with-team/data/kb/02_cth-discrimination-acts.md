## CHUNK: SDA-14
source: Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)
section: s 14(1)
title: Sex, pregnancy, marital status and family responsibilities discrimination in hiring
jurisdiction: Cth
topic: sex discrimination; pregnancy; family responsibilities; gender; hiring
keywords: sex, gender, woman, man, female, male, pregnancy, pregnant, potential pregnancy, maternity, breastfeeding, marital status, married, relationship status, family responsibilities, children, carer, parental leave, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status, offered employment
url: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02868/latest/text
accessed: 2026-10-20
---
Section 14(1) of the Sex Discrimination Act makes it unlawful for an employer, or anyone acting for an employer, to discriminate against a person on the ground of sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status, marital or relationship status, pregnancy or potential pregnancy, breastfeeding, or family responsibilities:
(a) in the arrangements made for deciding who should be offered employment;
(b) in deciding who should be offered employment; or
(c) in the terms or conditions on which employment is offered.

"Arrangements made for deciding who should be offered employment" includes the way applications are screened and scored. A screening tool is part of those arrangements.

The Act covers indirect discrimination as well as direct discrimination. Indirect discrimination is a condition that looks neutral but disadvantages people of one sex, or with family responsibilities, and is not reasonable. An example is penalising career breaks, which in practice are taken more often by women and by people with caring responsibilities.

## CHUNK: ADA-18

url: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A01302/latest/text
accessed: 2026-10-20
---
Section 18(1) of the Age Discrimination Act makes it unlawful for an employer to discriminate against a person on the ground of the person's age:
(a) in the arrangements made for deciding who should be offered employment;
(b) in deciding who should be offered employment; or
(c) in the terms or conditions on which employment is offered.

The Act protects people of any age, younger as well as older. Discrimination can be direct, such as preferring applicants "under 35". It can also be indirect, through a requirement that is not reasonable and that disadvantages an age group, such as unnecessary "recent graduate" or "young and energetic" requirements, or very long minimum experience periods.

An inherent requirements exception exists, but it is narrow. It must be a genuine requirement of the particular job, not a preference or a stereotype.

Practical meaning for screening: dates such as graduation year or year of first job must not be used to estimate age, and "years of experience" should not be treated as a stand-in for age.

## CHUNK: DDA-15

url: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A04426/latest/text
accessed: 2026-10-20
---
Section 15(1) of the Disability Discrimination Act makes it unlawful for an employer, or a person acting or purporting to act for an employer, to discriminate against a person on the ground of the person's disability:
(a) in the arrangements made for deciding who should be offered employment;
(b) in deciding who should be offered employment; or
(c) in the terms or conditions on which employment is offered.

"Disability" has a broad meaning. It includes physical, intellectual, psychiatric, sensory and neurological conditions, illness and injury. It covers disabilities a person has now, had in the past, may have in the future, or is assumed to have.

The Australian Human Rights Commission explains that discrimination includes treating a person with a disability less favourably than a person without the disability in similar circumstances. It also includes indirect discrimination through rules that apply to everyone but unfairly affect people with a particular disability.

Practical meaning for screening: a disclosed disability or health condition is not evidence about any selection criterion and must not lower any score.

## CHUNK: DDA-21A
source: Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth)
section: s 21A; ss 4, 5 (reasonable adjustments); s 11 (unjustifiable hardship)
title: Inherent requirements, reasonable adjustments and unjustifiable hardship
jurisdiction: Cth
topic: inherent requirements; reasonable adjustments; disability; accommodation
keywords: reasonable adjustments, adjustment request, accommodation, accessibility, inherent requirements, unjustifiable hardship, interview adjustments, assistive technology, flexible arrangements, ramp, screen reader, extra time
url: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A04426/latest/text
accessed: 2026-10-20
---
Under section 21A, it is not unlawful to decline to employ a person with a disability if, because of the disability, the person would be unable to carry out the inherent requirements of the particular work, even with reasonable adjustments. The adjustments are part of the test: the question is whether the person could do the inherent requirements with reasonable adjustments in place, not without them. In assessing this, the employer considers the person's past training, qualifications and experience relevant to the work, and any other relevant factor.

The Australian Human Rights Commission explains that employers "must consider how the person with a disability could be provided with reasonable adjustments". An adjustment is required unless it would impose "unjustifiable hardship" on the employer.

Practical meaning for screening: a resume that discloses a disability or asks for an adjustment, such as an accessible interview venue or extra time for a test, must be flagged so a recruiter can arrange the adjustment. It must never be scored down. A screening tool cannot judge inherent requirements or unjustifiable hardship; those questions go to a human.

## CHUNK: RDA-15
source: Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth)
section: s 15; s 9 (racial discrimination); Australian Human Rights Commission employer guidance
title: Racial discrimination in hiring - race, colour, descent, national or ethnic origin, immigrant status
jurisdiction: Cth
topic: racial discrimination; national origin; ethnic names; immigrant status; hiring
keywords: race, colour, descent, ethnic origin, national origin, ethnicity, name, surname, foreign name, accent, migrant, immigrant status, overseas born, cultural background, fit in, culture fit, refuse to employ
url: https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00274/latest/text
accessed: 2026-10-20
---
Section 15 of the Racial Discrimination Act makes it unlawful for an employer to refuse or fail to employ a person, or to offer a person less favourable terms, by reason of the person's race, colour, or national or ethnic origin.

The Australian Human Rights Commission's employer guidance says discrimination because of race, colour, descent, national or ethnic origin or immigrant status is unlawful "during recruitment and selection processes". It gives these examples of unlawful conduct:
- refusing to employ someone because of a generalisation about their racial group;
- not employing someone because of assumptions that they "wouldn't 'fit in'".

Two limits apply. The Act does not prohibit discrimination based solely on nationality or citizenship, and an employer is not required to employ someone without a valid work visa. Work rights are therefore a separate, lawful question for a recruiter to confirm.

Practical meaning for screening: a candidate's name, country of birth or language background must not affect any score. Identical resumes with different names should receive the same scores.
